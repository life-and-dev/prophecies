# Isaiah 40:3: A Voice in the Wilderness

Isaiah's call to prepare the LORD's way is applied to John the Baptist in the Gospels. This draft distinguishes that application from claims about Jesus' identity.

## The Prophecy

> “In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD; make straight in the desert a highway for our God.” — Isaiah 40:3 (ESV)

Isaiah 40 announces comfort for Zion after suffering. Isaiah 40:1-2 calls for Jerusalem to be comforted because her warfare has ended and her iniquity is pardoned. Jewish readings commonly locate this setting in the Babylonian exile and understand Isaiah 40-55 as a new Exodus: the LORD makes a way for the national return and restoration of Israel.

[Targum Jonathan on Isaiah 40](https://www.sefaria.org/Targum_Jonathan_on_Isaiah.40) renders the passage with the people of the LORD and the congregation of God, supporting a national-return reading. Matthew 3:1-3, Mark 1:2-3, Luke 3:4-6, and John 1:23 apply the voice in the wilderness to John the Baptist. The Gospels also distinguish John's call to repentance from his witness that another is coming.

## Christian View

The Gospels identify John as the voice and Jesus as the one whose way is prepared. Matthew 3:3, Mark 1:3, Luke 3:4, and John 1:23 each make this application. This is read as a YHWH-text applied to Jesus and therefore as evidence for Jesus' divine identity.

John's wilderness ministry fits the Gospel presentation: he wore camel's hair, ate locusts and wild honey, preached repentance, and baptised those responding to that call. He denies that he is the Christ (John 1:20; Luke 3:15-16) and points to Jesus as God's Lamb (John 1:29). Luke 3:15-16 says people wondered whether John was the Christ, but John said one mightier than he was coming, that he was unworthy to untie his sandals, and that Jesus baptises with the Holy Spirit and fire. John never calls Jesus God in this testimony. Christians understand this as spiritual preparation for redemption through Jesus. They may regard it as God visiting his people through the Son, not merely a change of subject.

## Non-Christian / Secular / Jewish Views

Jewish readings locate Isaiah 40 in the physical restoration of Israel from the Babylonian exile. The LORD's way is prepared for God's own saving action and for the return of the exiles, a national restoration expressed through new-Exodus imagery. See [Babylonian exile context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity).

A Unitarian reading accepts the Gospel application while distinguishing agency from identity: John prepares Israel for God's Messiah, the one whom God sent. This makes the Christian focus spiritual redemption through Christ, while the Jewish focus remains Israel's physical restoration. John 1:6,33 and 2 Corinthians 5:19 distinguish God as sender and reconciler from Christ as the one through whom reconciliation occurs. The Synoptic applications can therefore be read as fulfilment through an authorised representative rather than as a direct statement that Jesus is YHWH.

## Conclusion

All views can recognise the Gospel application of Isaiah's voice to John. Christian interpretation sees John preparing for Jesus and spiritual redemption, with a deity inference from the LORD-text; Jewish interpretation sees the [national return and restoration](#the-prophecy), while an agency reading identifies Jesus as the sent Messiah.
