# Psalm 109:8: May Another Take His Office

Acts 1 cites Psalms 69 and 109 when the apostles replace Judas. This draft distinguishes David's psalm context from later apostolic application.

## The Prophecy

> “May his days be few; may another take his office!” — Psalm 109:8 (ESV)

Psalm 109 is attributed to David and is an imprecatory prayer against accusers who repay his love with hatred. Psalm 69 is likewise a Davidic lament. Neither text names Judas or gives an explicit future prediction about him.

Acts 1:15-20 cites both Psalm 69:25, “May his camp become desolate,” and Psalm 109:8 before Matthias is chosen to fill Judas's office. Peter therefore uses two Psalms in the same decision.

## Christian View

Acts 1:15-22 explicitly cites Psalm 69:25 and Psalm 109:8 when the apostles replace Judas with a resurrection witness. Christians therefore read the two Davidic Psalms as fulfilled in Judas's betrayal, death, and replacement. The vacant *pekudah* is understood as Judas's apostolic office.

The correspondence is read as more than a general principle: Judas's rejection of Jesus, the vacancy among the Twelve, and Peter's appeal to Scripture are treated as intended fulfilment. The severe judgement language is understood as divine judgement on betrayal.

## Non-Christian / Secular / Jewish Views

Psalm 109 is David's imprecatory prayer against historical accusers who lie, repay love with hatred, and pursue the poor and needy. Its curses include a widow, fatherless children, loss of property, remembered family sin, and curses on the enemy. The full portrait does not closely describe Judas, and Jesus' commands to love enemies and forgive persecutors (Matthew 5:44; Luke 23:34) differ from the psalm's curse language.

On this reading, Peter applies a scriptural principle to Judas's vacant position rather than claiming that David predicted Judas. *Pekudah* means position, oversight, or responsibility, not specifically apostleship. Critical readings likewise see Acts using Scripture after the event as theological application. Psalm 69 and Psalm 109 remain Davidic prayers, not explicit messianic predictions.

## Conclusion

Acts 1 deliberately applies Psalms 69 and 109 to Judas and the vacant office. Christian readings treat that use as fulfilment; Jewish, secular, and contextual readings treat it as application of Davidic lament to a new event. The different conclusions depend on whether apostolic application establishes predictive intent beyond the [Davidic context](#the-prophecy).
