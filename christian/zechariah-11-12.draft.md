# Zechariah 11:12: Thirty Pieces of Silver

Zechariah's shepherd drama includes thirty pieces of silver and a potter. Matthew connects related details with Judas and the potter's field.

## The Prophecy

> “They weighed out as my wages thirty pieces of silver. Then the LORD said to me, ‘Throw it to the potter.’” — Zechariah 11:12-13 (ESV)

Zechariah 11 is a complete shepherding drama. The LORD tells Zechariah, “Shepherd the flock doomed to slaughter” (11:4). The KJV calls the speaker “the LORD my God”, and the first-person speech later in the chapter has led some Trinitarians to infer that the rejected shepherd shares YHWH's identity. That inference depends on interpretation; the text also moves among the LORD, Zechariah, the flock, and shepherd figures.

The sequence is: Zechariah shepherds the flock with staffs called Favour and Union; removes three shepherds in one month; ends his care for a mutually hostile flock; breaks Favour to annul a covenant; receives thirty pieces of silver after asking for his wages; throws the silver in the house of the LORD to the potter; then breaks Union, the brotherhood of Judah and Israel (11:7-14). The chapter closes with a worthless or idol shepherd whose arm withers and right eye is blinded (11:15-17).

The KJV's “a goodly price that I was prised at of them” is ironic in context. A cited “yet” in paraphrase or argument is not a separate KJV word in this passage and can affect the perceived contrast; translations also differ over whether the Hebrew *yotzer* means “potter” or, by a proposed reading related to *otzar*, “treasury”. Exodus 21:32 fixes thirty shekels as compensation for a slave killed by an ox, which makes the wage a sign of contempt or disgust rather than honour.

## Christian View

Matthew 26:14-16 records Judas receiving thirty pieces of silver. Matthew 27:3-10 records his return of the money in the temple and the purchase of a potter's field. Christians identify three close correspondences: the amount, the temple setting, and the potter or potter's field. The slave-price association in Exodus 21:32 further supports their reading of the payment as a degrading valuation of Jesus.

On this reading, the whole drama supplies a type and its antitype. Zechariah's rejected shepherd and contemptuous payment foreshadow Jesus' rejection and Judas's role. The remaining details are not identical: Zechariah concerns sheep, three shepherds, a broken covenant, Judah and Israel, and an idol shepherd, whereas Matthew concerns Judas. Zechariah's final arm-and-eye judgement is not the same event as Judas's hanging.

The Zechariah-not-Jeremiah objection arises because Matthew attributes fulfilment wording to Jeremiah rather than Zechariah. Christian explanations appeal to Isaiah's potter and LORD imagery in Isaiah 45:9-13, Jeremiah's potter imagery in Jeremiah 18:1-6, and Jeremiah's field and judgement material in Jeremiah 19 and 32; others propose that Jeremiah functioned as a heading for a prophetic collection. These explanations treat Matthew's citation as a composite or thematic attribution, not necessarily a verbatim quotation of Zechariah alone.

## Non-Christian / Secular / Jewish Views

Jewish readings treat the chapter as Zechariah's symbolic drama about rejected divine shepherding in its post-exilic setting. Favour and Union can represent divine favour and the unity or brotherhood of Judah and Israel. The staffs, covenant consequences, sheep, shepherds, and ambiguous *yotzer* reference belong to that drama; it contains no explicit Messiah or Judas.

Critical readings likewise describe Matthew's use as midrashic or typological application. On this account, Matthew combines Zechariah's silver and potter language with Jeremiah's potter, field, and judgement imagery, rather than presenting one-to-one prediction. Isaiah 45:9-13 and Jeremiah 18:1-6 also use potter and LORD imagery, but do not themselves supply the Judas narrative. For the potter material applied to Matthew, see also [Zechariah 11's Christian discussion](#christian-view).

Matthew's account differs in details: Judas hangs himself in Matthew 27:5, whereas Acts 1:18 describes his fatal fall. Proposed harmonisations exist, but the accounts do not state the same manner of death. The arm-and-eye judgement on the idol shepherd is likewise distinct from hanging; the sheep, three shepherds, broken covenant, and Judah-Israel division have no direct counterparts in Matthew's Judas account.

## Conclusion

The Christian view regards Judas, silver, temple, and potter as fulfilment or typology. Jewish and critical views regard them as later application of a [shepherding drama](#non-christian-secular-jewish-views). The original context does not explicitly name the Messiah.
