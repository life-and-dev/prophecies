# Jeremiah 23:5-6 (The Righteous Branch)

Jeremiah 23:5–6 promises a righteous ruler from David's line who will establish justice, save Judah, and secure Israel. The passage itself states these promises and its identification of the ruler and timing of their fulfilment are matters of interpretation.

> "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when **I will raise up for David a righteous Branch**, and **he shall reign as king and deal wisely**, and shall execute justice and righteousness in the land. In his days **Judah will be saved, and Israel will dwell securely**. And this is the name by which he will be called: **'The LORD is our righteousness.'"** — Jeremiah 23:5-6 (ESV)

Christians identify this ruler with Jesus. Traditional Jewish readings await the promise's fulfilment. Historical-critical scholars commonly situate the oracle in late-monarchic or exilic hopes for restoration. This article compares these readings without treating any one identification as settled by the passage alone.

Jeremiah 23:7–8 adds restoration imagery relevant to the comparison:

> "Therefore, behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when they shall no longer say, 'As the LORD lives who brought up the people of Israel out of the land of Egypt,' but 'As the LORD lives who brought up and led the offspring of the house of Israel out of the north country and out of all the countries where he had driven them.' Then they shall dwell in their own land." — Jeremiah 23:7-8 (ESV)

## Interpretations

### Christian View

Many Christians identify Jesus as the "righteous Branch" from David's line. Matthew traces Jesus' genealogy through Joseph and presents Him as son of David (Matthew 1:1-16). Luke's genealogy is traditionally associated by some Christians with Mary, although this interpretation is disputed (Luke 3:23-31). Acts calls Jesus "the Righteous One" (Acts 3:14; 7:52), while 1 Peter and 1 John describe Him as righteous (1 Peter 3:18; 1 John 2:1). Believers are declared righteous through faith in Him (Romans 3:21-26; 1 Corinthians 1:30; 2 Corinthians 5:21). These texts present Jesus as exalted at God's right hand and exercising present lordship (Acts 2:33-36; Philippians 2:9-11). "Spiritual reign" is a Christian theological description. He has also been appointed by God to judge the living and the dead (Acts 10:42; 17:31; John 5:22,27). Most Christians acknowledge that the physical, national promises of salvation and security for Judah and Israel await His second coming.

### Jewish View

Jeremiah 23:5-6 is traditionally understood in many Jewish interpretations as a promise of a future Messiah, a righteous Davidic king who will restore Israel. [Maimonides' messianic criteria](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Kings_and_Wars.11.1-4?lang=bi) include a Davidic king who restores Torah observance, rebuilds the Temple, and gathers Israel's dispersed people. A [Jewish overview of the Messiah](https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/the-messiah) likewise contrasts these expectations with Christian claims about Jesus.

From this perspective, the promised political and national security did not occur during or after Jesus' lifetime. Israel does not dwell securely, Jesus did not rule as a political king in Jerusalem, and the return from exile described in Jeremiah 23:7-8 has not occurred in its complete form. Many traditional Jewish interpretations therefore do not identify Jesus as the Messiah because they expect these promises to be fulfilled visibly and completely.

### Secular Historical-Critical View

Historical-critical scholarship reads the oracle in the setting of the late Judean monarchy, the Babylonian crisis and exile, and hopes for restoration. The [SBL Bible Odyssey overview of Jeremiah](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/jeremiah/) and [Yale Bible Study course on Jeremiah and Ezekiel](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/jeremiah-and-ezekiel/) situate Jeremiah's message within these historical contexts. In this approach, the "Branch" is an unspecified future Davidic ruler who embodies restoration hope, not a figure textually identified as Jesus. Historical-critical interpretation assesses the passage's historical meaning rather than whether it has a supernatural fulfilment.

### Comparative Questions and Interpretations

#### 1. Was Judah Saved?

**Jewish interpretation:** Many traditional Jewish interpretations take "Judah will be saved" (verse 6) to mean political and national deliverance. Judah remained under Rome during Jesus' life, and Roman forces destroyed Jerusalem and the Second Temple in 70 CE, followed by further Jewish dispersal. That promised deliverance did not occur.

**Christian interpretation:** Many Christians understand Jesus to bring spiritual salvation, citing Jewish believers in Acts 2:41; 4:4; 21:20 and forgiveness, reconciliation, and eternal life. They acknowledge that national salvation remains future, reading Romans 11:25-27 and Zechariah 12:10; 14:1-9 in relation to Christ's return. The meaning and scope of "all Israel" in Romans 11:25-27 are disputed. In Zechariah 12:10, the pierced figure and pronouns are textually disputed; national salvation is inferred rather than directly stated. Jesus links Jerusalem's rejection with desolation and future recognition (Matthew 23:37-39; Luke 19:41-44). Kingdom postponement is one Christian theological inference from these texts.

#### 2. Does Israel Dwell Securely?

**Traditional Jewish interpretation:** Many traditional Jewish interpretations take "Israel will dwell securely" (verse 6) as physical safety, not spiritual peace. The Hebrew *betach* (בֶּטַח) denotes security. Jerusalem's destruction in 70 CE, subsequent persecution, and continuing conflict show that complete security has not occurred.

**Christian interpretation:** Many Christians distinguish eternal security in Jesus (John 10:28-29) from the future physical security promised for Israel. They read Ezekiel 34:25-28 as a promise of future security in the Messianic Age after Christ's return. Thus, complete physical security remains future in their two-comings framework.

#### 3. Where Is the Physical Kingdom?

**Traditional Jewish interpretation:** Many traditional Jewish interpretations take "he shall reign as king" (verse 5) as a visible political reign. Jesus did not rule Israel from Jerusalem and was executed by Rome; no visible kingdom followed.

**Christian interpretation:** Many Christians say Jesus reigns now at God's right hand (Acts 2:33-36; Philippians 2:9-11), while His kingdom is not from this world in origin or method (John 18:36). Some read Revelation 20:4-6 and Zechariah 14:9,16-17 as a future visible reign after the Second Coming. Revelation 20:4-6 explicitly describes a thousand-year reign, but its earthly location, visibility, chronology, and literal duration are disputed among Christians.

#### 4. What About the Return from Exile?

**Traditional Jewish interpretation:** Jeremiah 23:7-8 changes the remembrance formula from the exodus to the LORD's bringing Israel back from the north country and all countries of dispersion. Reading this as a regathering greater than the exodus is an interpretation. The partial return from Babylon around 538 BC, before Jesus, did not fulfil that complete gathering; Jesus did not gather dispersed Israel.

**Christian interpretation:** Many Christians likewise regard the Babylonian return and modern Israel's re-establishment in 1948 as incomplete. They cite Isaiah 11:1-12 and Ezekiel 37:21-24 for a final gathering under one Davidic king, and Matthew 24:31 for gathering the elect from the four winds. Identifying the elect in Matthew 24:31 as national Israel is interpretive. In this view, completion awaits Christ's return.

#### 5. Why Divide Political Restoration into Two Comings?

**Traditional Jewish interpretation:** Many traditional Jewish interpretations read Jeremiah 23 as one promise: God replaces leaders who scattered Israel with the Messiah, who gathers and restores the nation. The passage concerns political restoration, not spiritual salvation alone, and does not describe stages separated by millennia.

**Christian interpretation:** Many Christians resolve this through two comings: the suffering servant brings spiritual salvation (Isaiah 53), and the conquering king brings physical restoration (Revelation 19). Linking Isaiah 53 and Revelation 19 to two comings is a Christian synthesis. They hold that New Testament revelation distinguishes these stages, although the prophets present Messiah's work together. This is a Christian theological framework, not the only reading of Jeremiah.

#### 6. Does "The LORD Is Our Righteousness" Prove Jesus Is God?

**Traditional Jewish interpretation:** Many traditional Jewish interpretations take the title as a declaration of Israel's righteous and secure condition under the Messiah. [Jerusalem receives the same name in Jeremiah 33:16](https://biblehub.com/jeremiah/33-16.htm), so the name need not identify the Messiah as God. Similar divine-name place and altar names support this reading (Genesis 22:14; Exodus 17:15; Judges 6:24).

**Christian interpretation:** Some Christian commentators call the title "[an eminent proof of the Godhead of Christ](https://biblehub.com/commentaries/jeremiah/23-6.htm)," because it uses [YHWH (יְהוָה)](https://ofgod.info/name) and identifies the Messiah as righteousness' source. However, the title does not grammatically prove that the ruler is YHWH. These commentators say Jerusalem receives this name because the Messiah gives it righteousness.

## Conclusion

[Christian readings](#christian-view) commonly identify Jesus with the Davidic Branch and await future completion of the prophecy's national dimensions. Christian interpretation is not uniform.

[Traditional Jewish readings](#jewish-view) generally do not accept Jesus as Messiah, holding that the visible criteria remain unmet. Jewish interpretation is likewise not uniform.

[Historical-critical readings](#secular-historical-critical-view) commonly understand the oracle as restoration hope for an unspecified Davidic ruler in Judah's crisis, rather than an identification of Jesus.
